Two power domains, joined only by ground

The single decision that shapes everything else: one 24 V supply, permanently powered, upstream of the contactor. Everything that must survive an e‑stop hangs off it — the Pi through a DC-DC, the console, and the Octopus's logic. The e‑stop drops the motors and the spindle and leaves the computer alive.

The Octopus is the interesting case, because it belongs to both domains. Its logic and endstops sit on the unswitched 24 V rail and stay live; only its VM stepper rail is switched. That is deliberate — see the e‑stop chain section for why dropping the board's logic is the wrong move on this machine.

The Octopus can power a Pi — it has a dedicated header (J26) and an 8 A 5 V buck, so this isn't a capacity problem. It's a behaviour problem. Hang the Pi off that rail and every e‑stop, every power-down and every fault kills the computer mid-cut: lost machine position, no controlled recovery, and an unclean NVMe shutdown every single time.

Wiring consequence

With two supplies, connect ground and the SPI signals only. Do not bridge 5 V between the Pi and the Octopus — two supplies fighting over one rail is how you cook a regulator. Bond the grounds properly; SPI needs a solid reference, not an incidental one.

Why a DC-DC rather than a second USB-C brick: 5 V/5 A is not a standard USB-PD profile. The standard fixed offers are 5/9/15/20 V at 3 A, so most chargers — even 100 W ones — give only 5 V/3 A and deliver their headline wattage at voltages the Pi cannot use. On 3 A it caps total USB current at 600 mA — awkward when the NVMe HAT is already drawing its own 2–5 W.

Signal chain

Step generation happens on the Octopus, not the Pi. The Pi runs the 1 kHz servo thread and one SPI transfer per cycle — which is why a Pi is the right host here and an x86 box isn't.

Full EXP2 pinout, odd pins in the left column: 1 PA6 · 2 PA5 · 3 PB1 · 4 PA4 · 5 PB2 · 6 PA7 · 7 PC15 · 8 RST · 9 GND · 10 PC5. Pins 3, 5, 8 and 10 are unused here.

Pin 8 is not the PRU reset

EXP2-8 is RST, the STM32's hardware reset. The PRU reset is EXP2-7 (PC_15). They are physically adjacent. Landing GPIO 25 one row over holds the whole MCU in reset — and the symptom is indistinguishable from a dead link: rp1lib initialises cleanly, claims its pins, and nothing ever answers.

EXP2 carries no 5 V at all — pin 10 is PC5. (Several widely-copied third-party pin tables say 5 V; BTT's own pinout says PC5. It is EXP1 that has 5 V on pin 10.) So the SPI harness cannot bridge the two power domains, which is one fewer thing to get wrong.

Why these pins were free

BTT labels PA_7/PA_6/PA_5 as Motor-SPI — the bus for SPI-mode stepper drivers such as the TMC2130 or 5160. This machine runs TMC2209s in UART mode, so that bus is idle and Remora gets it. Worth recording: moving to SPI-mode drivers later would collide head-on with the Remora link.

Serial debug — on the TFT header, not EXP2

The Octopus narrates its entire startup over UART, including whether it accepted its config.txt. Those lines are on the TFT header: RST · PA10 (RX) · PA9 (TX) · GND · 5V. This is the fastest way to tell "not powered" from "running but misconfigured", which the Pi side alone cannot distinguish.

TX and RX cross over. Pi pins 8, 9 and 10 are contiguous, so this is one 3‑pin block sitting just above the SPI block at 19–26.

The TFT 5 V pin is the one that can destroy something

Unlike EXP2, the TFT header does carry 5 V, and a stock 4‑wire TFT cable includes it. It must never land on the Pi header. The Pi has its own supply; a second 5 V source fed into it can kill the board. Cut that wire back or tape it off.

It also matters in the other direction: if the Octopus was being back‑fed 5 V through this pin, removing the wire leaves it unpowered, and the SPI link stays dead however correct the other wires are. Power the Octopus properly from 24 V, or from a USB cable during bench work.

Enabling the Pi's UART for this

On a Pi 5, /dev/serial0 maps to ttyAMA10 — the dedicated 3‑pin debug connector, not GPIO 14/15. So the kernel console does not occupy the header pins, and freeing them is not necessary. GPIO 14/15 simply are not enabled by default; add dtoverlay=uart0-pi5 to config.txt and reboot to get /dev/ttyAMA0. Read it with stty -F /dev/ttyAMA0 115200 raw -echo && cat /dev/ttyAMA0.

Verify against BTT's pinout, not the silkscreen

BIGTREETECH's own wiki warns that "the silkscreen on the first production run of the octopus had incorrectly labeled pins." Confirm EXP2 pin 1 by the square pad or the triangle marker, and trust the pinout image in the hardware repo over what is printed on the board.

EMI — this one bites late

Keep the SPI ribbon physically away from the spindle motor cable, and keep it short. VFD switching noise coupling into SPI is a leading cause of Remora comms errors, and it presents as a software problem — random link drops, e‑stops mid-program — which is exactly the wrong place to go looking. Use shielded cable for the spindle motor run and bond the shield at the VFD end only; grounding both ends invites a loop.

How the speed signal actually gets there

Worth knowing before wiring, because it isn't what you would guess: Remora exposes no PWM pin to LinuxCNC at all. The component creates only SPI-*, PRU-reset, joint.N.*, input.NN, output.NN, SP.N and PV.N.

Spindle speed therefore travels on a setpoint channel: LinuxCNC writes remora.SP.0, and the firmware's PWM module consumes that index and drives the pin. The firmware-side block, with keys read out of the flashed binary:

The enable relay is a Digital Pin in Output mode, reaching HAL as remora.output.00. Note the two digits — a one-digit name silently fails to bind rather than erroring.

Verify before you land these

Those designations come from the Milo documentation, which assumes a Mitsubishi‑style terminal layout. VFD terminal numbering varies by manufacturer. On many drives one of the low-numbered analog terminals is a 10 V reference output, not an input — wiring your 0–10 V signal into it shorts an output against a source. Check your drive's own manual and confirm which terminal is the analog input before connecting anything.

E-stop chain

The e‑stop breaks the contactor coil circuit directly, in copper. LinuxCNC finding out about it is awareness, not the safety function — never make software the thing that stops the machine.

As built today — 2026-09-06

There is no contactor in the machine. The wiring is mains AC → e-stop switch → 24 V PSU, with the VFD on the same switched side. The mushroom's own contacts break mains directly.

That is a valid category-0 stop and nothing about it is unsafe. Press it and the spindle and the steppers both lose power. Everything below is about what it can't do, not about a hazard.

It has three limits, and the first is the one that matters:

  • It cannot grow a second station. With the mushroom breaking mains, any additional e‑stop — a pendant, a second panel button — would have to break mains too. Running mains down a pendant tether is a non-starter, so the current topology structurally forbids a pendant e‑stop.
  • It kills the Octopus. Dropping the 24 V PSU takes the board's logic with it, and an abnormal power loss is exactly what leaves the Octopus wedged in WDRESET needing octopus-reset.sh. Every press is a chance to wedge the board — see the WDRESET section in README-config.md.
  • It wears the contacts. A 22 mm mushroom contact block is rated for control circuits. It is currently breaking a 1.5 kW VFD's mains, inrush included, every time.

The rewire — three moves

  1. 24 V PSU moves upstream of the contactor, permanently powered. It now supplies the coil circuit itself, so the e‑stop cannot kill the supply that has to re-energise it.
  2. A 24 VDC-coil contactor switches the VFD's mains and the Octopus's VM stepper rail. Spindle and motors dead on press — the actual safety requirement.
  3. Octopus logic and endstops stay live. No WDRESET wedge, and LinuxCNC stays coherent enough to say why it stopped instead of dumping you into a link-down diagnosis.

Every e‑stop button then sits in series in the 24 V coil loop, carrying tens of milliamps. Adding the pendant becomes two more NC contacts in that loop — the tether never carries mains. A momentary START in parallel with the contactor's NO auxiliary gives the seal-in latch, so power never returns on its own.

Stage 2, drop-in later

A safety relay between the button chain and the coil buys dual-channel monitoring, cross-fault detection, force-guided contacts and EDM (the contactor's NC auxiliary loops back, so a welded contactor blocks the reset). That is the piece that makes a detachable e‑stop trustworthy, because the failure you actually care about in a pendant is a chafed conductor in a cable that gets flexed daily — and dual-channel is what detects it. Note it replaces the seal-in latch; its monitored reset does that job. Pull four e‑stop conductors in the tether from day one even if stage 1 only uses two.

Software path: an auxiliary NC contact on the contactor into a spare Octopus input, so LinuxCNC knows the machine is dead and refuses to run. octopus/config.txt currently defines only four digital inputs (three endstops plus the toolsetter), so there is room. This is for state, not for stopping.

Free watchdog: Remora's SPI-status pin already drops LinuxCNC's enable if the link to the Octopus fails. It's wired in milo.hal — keep it.

Still open — spindle braking

Killing mains to a VFD while the spindle is turning is hard on the drive, and the spindle then coasts for a long time with a tool in the work. The H100 has X1–X6 digital inputs with a P12 source, so one can be assigned to external-fault or free-stop and driven from the safety relay's instantaneous output, with the contactor dropping on a delayed output behind it.

🚨 The H100 has no STO. X1–X6 are functional inputs, not safety-rated ones. Anything done through them is a courtesy to the drive, never the safety function — the contactor stays the stop. Whether you want coast (category 0) or a commanded decel then power-off (category 1) is still undecided.

Two things to check before ordering the contactor

It needs a normally-open auxiliary contact for the LinuxCNC feedback into remora.input.04. The MC-9b listing does not mention one — either find a variant with an integrated aux or budget for a clip-on block.

Three poles is the right count: VFD line, VFD neutral, and the 24 V VM rail. The Heschen is two-pole, so it is cheaper but you would need two of them, and modular lighting contactors usually have no aux contact at all. The UL listing on the MC-9b is worth the difference in a shared workshop.

Sizing, settled

9 A AC-3 sounds small next to a 1.5 kW drive, but a contactor downstream of a breaker never carries more than the breaker allows — the circuit protection sizes it, not the nameplate. On a 15 A circuit, the MC-9b's ~25 A AC-1 rating has 60 % headroom. The spindle is air-cooled and small endmills in aluminium draw a few hundred watts, so the drive is simply oversized.

Before you cut a wire

Nine items. Four are settled, five are not — and the split matters, because everything still open needs the physical board in front of you.

01Octopus SPI pins ResolvedPA_7 / PA_6 / PA_5 / PA_4 to Pi 19/21/23/24. Link proven, and the component was watched claiming those GPIOs as it initialised.
02Driver modules ResolvedTMC2209, pulled from the old board. Now configured over UART rather than left at defaults — which is what gets them out of StealthChop.
03Mains voltage for the VFD Resolved110 V single-phase in, three-phase out. One ordinary house circuit through the contactor; no 240 V feed to design around.
04Spindle PWM schema ResolvedSpeed goes out on remora.SP.0; the firmware PWM module takes SP / PWM Pin / PWM Max / Hardware PWM. Read out of the flashed firmware, so it is right for this binary.
05The PRU reset wire OpenPi GPIO 25 (pin 22) to Octopus PC_15. Hardcoded in the component, so it appears in no config file. Confirm the wire is physically present.
06Endstop pins and polarity OpenAssigned PG_6 / PG_9 / PG_10 on DIAG0–2, reaching HAL as remora.input.00/01/02. Those pin numbers come from the standard board pinout, not Remora's docs. Check the silkscreen, then check polarity in halshow with motors unpowered.
07TMC UART pins OpenPC_4 / PD_11 / PC_6, again from the standard pinout. A wrong UART pin fails silently — the driver simply keeps its defaults, so you would be back on StealthChop at the wrong current with nothing complaining. Watch the boot output.
08VFD make, model and terminals OpenStill the one place where a guess can destroy hardware. It also decides analog-plus-relay versus Modbus, which is a fork in the wiring rather than a setting.
09Spindle at-speed OpenThere is no signal, and without one G-code plunges the moment it issues M3 — before the spindle has spooled up. Wants the VFD's "up to frequency" output into a Remora input, or Modbus if the drive turns out to be a Huanyang.
